OpenRTS · Development Plan

Road to Warcraft

What separates the current build from a real-time strategy game people would choose to play — and the order to close it in.

✦

Where it actually stands

The economy loop is complete and tested: workers gather gold and lumber, nine buildings place, construct, train and upgrade through ten painted tiers each, and land, sea and air units path around terrain that suits them. The simulation is deterministic and command-driven, which is the expensive architectural decision most hobby RTS projects get wrong and have to redo.

That is roughly the first third of an RTS, and it is the least fun third. What exists is a city-builder with no opponent.

What "as good as Warcraft" really means

Not the art — yours is already better than Warcraft II's. The gap is that Warcraft is a game about decisions under pressure: an opponent forces you to choose between economy and army, your units respond crisply enough that losing feels like your fault, and the map hides information so scouting matters. None of that exists yet. Phases 1 through 4 below are not polish — they are the game.

1

Combat and death

Large · The single biggest unlock

Until units can kill each other there is no game, only a diorama. Everything after this phase is refinement; this phase is the difference between a toy and a game.

  • Attack damage, armour, range and cooldown in data/units.ts; damage resolution and death in World.step().
  • Acquisition: idle units auto-attack hostiles that come within range, and attack-move as an order type alongside move.
  • Projectiles for archers and bombers — travel time matters, instant hits feel cheap.
  • Unit collision and separation. This is the one you cannot skip: units currently stack on one tile, and armies will read as a blob without it.
  • Wire the Foundry's armour and the Watch Tower's radius into real combat maths rather than the placeholder HP bonus and vision circle they drive today.
  • Building damage, rubble and a defeat condition.
Done when: two roughly equal armies meet, one wins, and watching it happen tells you which side played better.
2

Fog of war

Medium · Makes scouting a decision

Perfect information kills strategy — you cannot bluff, raid or be surprised. Fog is also what makes the Watch Tower worth building.

  • Per-player visibility grid updated from unit and building sight radii; unexplored, explored-but-unseen, and visible states.
  • Remembered terrain and buildings under the shroud; units hidden until seen.
  • Renderer draws the shroud as a cached overlay so it costs one blit, not a per-tile pass.
  • The minimap must respect fog, or it leaks everything the fog just hid.
Done when: you genuinely do not know what your opponent is building without sending someone to look.
3

An opponent worth beating

Large · Where single-player lives or dies

A scripted AI that gathers, expands and attacks on a timer is enough to make the game playable. An AI that reacts to what it scouts is what makes it replayable.

  • Build-order executor: a declarative opening per difficulty, driven through the same Command queue a human uses — never by mutating state directly.
  • Worker allocation that rebalances gold and lumber against what the build order needs.
  • Attack waves with rally, regroup and retreat-when-losing behaviour.
  • Difficulty through better decisions and earlier timings, not resource cheats — players can always feel a cheating AI, and it teaches them nothing.
  • Expansion to a second gold mine when the first runs low.
Done when: the medium AI beats you the first time you play, and you can explain exactly what it did.
4

Feel and control

Medium · Cheap to build, enormous payoff

This is the phase most amateur RTS projects skip, and the reason they feel amateur. Warcraft's controls are invisible; yours are still noticeable.

  • Control groups (Ctrl+1–9), double-click select-all-of-type, box-select respecting unit priority, and Tab to cycle subgroups.
  • Idle-worker key. Nothing else you build will save players as much time.
  • Order feedback: click markers, waypoint queueing with Shift, audible and visual acknowledgement that a command registered.
  • Sound. Selection barks, combat, construction, and a "your building is under attack" alert. Silence is the loudest thing missing right now.
  • Smooth unit turning and acceleration instead of instant facing changes.
  • Fix the hotkey collision: with a worker selected, S and W build instead of panning the camera.
Done when: you stop noticing the controls.
5

Depth: tech, counters and a second faction

Large · Turns one match into a hundred

Ten building tiers is progression, but it is not yet strategy — every tier is strictly better than the last, so there is no choice, only a queue. Choice comes from options that beat each other.

  • A counter triangle: infantry beat archers, archers beat flyers, flyers beat siege. Armour classes and damage types in the data tables, not in code.
  • Branching upgrades where a tier makes you pick one of two, so two players' tech paths differ.
  • Give the Clans their own units and building art. They currently reuse Human sprites with a colour tint, which is the most visible remaining shortcut.
  • Hero units or a signature ability per faction — Warcraft III's real innovation, and what makes matches memorable rather than merely balanced.
  • Population cap tuning so armies have a natural ceiling and composition matters.
Done when: two good players open differently and both openings are defensible.
6

Maps and modes

Medium
  • Hand-authored maps in a simple JSON format, plus a map editor — even a crude one, since you will otherwise author maps by editing numbers.
  • Better generation: guaranteed-fair mirrored starts, chokepoints, expansion sites and verified path connectivity between every start pair.
  • A short campaign. Missions with objectives teach the game far better than a tutorial popup, and are where your art will shine.
  • Victory conditions beyond annihilation, and a post-match summary screen.
7

Multiplayer

Large · Cheaper than usual, because of a choice you already made

The sim was built deterministic and command-driven from day one, so lockstep is an addition rather than a rewrite. Do not start it until Phase 1 is finished — netcode over a game with no combat tests nothing.

  • Prove determinism first. A state-hash test that replays a seed and command log and compares hashes every tick. Until that exists, lockstep will desync and you will have no way to find out why.
  • Move facing out of sim state, or replace Math.atan2 — its results are not guaranteed identical across JavaScript engines.
  • Lobby and matchmaking on Supabase; command relay over WebRTC with a WebSocket fallback.
  • Turn-delay lockstep with input buffering, plus a desync detector that dumps both hashes rather than silently diverging.
  • Replays come free once the command log is authoritative — record it, ship it.
Done when: a 20-minute game between two machines ends with identical state hashes.
8

Production polish

Medium
  • Serve sprites as separate cached files instead of inlining 90 base64 PNGs into one 4.5 MB HTML file; add a loading screen and lazy-load higher tiers.
  • Retina rendering — the canvas currently ignores devicePixelRatio and looks soft on good screens.
  • Performance pass at 400+ units: spatial hash for target acquisition, dirty-rect or batched drawing, flow fields instead of per-unit A*.
  • Settings: hotkey rebinding, volume, scroll speed, resolution.
  • Save and load a match, which falls out of the command log almost free.

Three things to keep doing throughout

These are not phases; they are habits that decide whether the phases stay cheap.

Guard the sim boundary

Nothing outside sim/ may mutate state except by issuing a Command. Every shortcut here costs you multiplayer and replays later.

Tune in data, not code

Balance belongs in data/ tables. If changing a unit's damage means editing a function, balancing the game becomes an engineering task and you will do it less.

Extend the headless playtest

It already drives real sessions and catches real bugs. Add a case with every feature — it is the only thing that will let you refactor combat without fear.

What I would cut

Scope is the thing that kills projects like this, and it usually arrives disguised as ambition.

  • More buildings and tiers. Nine buildings at ten tiers is already more content than Warcraft II shipped with. Adding a tenth building is the most enjoyable way to avoid writing combat.
  • 3D, or an engine port. Your art is 2D isometric and it looks excellent. A port buys nothing and costs everything.
  • Ranked ladders, accounts, cosmetics. These serve a player base you do not have yet.
  • Perfect balance before Phase 3. You cannot balance a game you cannot play against an opponent. Ship rough numbers and fix them with real matches.

The honest summary

Phases 1 to 4 are the game, and they are perhaps two-thirds of the remaining work to something genuinely fun. Phase 5 is what makes it worth replaying. Phases 6 to 8 are what make it worth releasing. If you only ever finish Phase 1, you will have a real RTS; if you finish Phase 4, you will have one that feels good to play — and that, more than content volume, is what people actually mean when they say a game is as good as Warcraft.
